# Round 1 - Opening positions

## Graeber
SEAT: Graeber (Debt, money, and social relations)
STANCE: Yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 8/10 - B 5/10 - C 6/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10 (Money as social relation: option A, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Open with the barter fable's demolition (option A); a student who still believes money began as truck and barter will hear everything after it, Golumbia included, through the fable.
WHY:
1. The sequence is the argument: credit and ledgers precede coinage by millennia, so the ground must be cleared before any coin, digital or otherwise, can be seen clearly (The myth of barter, S001).
2. Bitcoin is a ledger trying to behave like bullion; students cannot feel that contradiction until they hold both terms, and the credit term has to come first because it is the one history supports (Credit versus bullion, S002).
3. Golumbia's genealogy is stronger, not weaker, after the demolition: the goldbug romance he traces is precisely the bullion fantasy, and the class will recognize it on sight.
THE RISK I SEE: My material is lecture-only, so a Graeber-led lecture puts the frame in the instructor's voice and the assigned text in the supporting role; students may take the frame as settled because nobody made them read it.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Evidence that students meet the barter fable as a live belief they defend, rather than a story they have merely absorbed; then it needs no demolition and Golumbia can open.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Write the ninety-second version: one Mesopotamian tablet, one missing barter village, one question to the room.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: When you say Golumbia anchors the assigned side, will students be examined on his argument or on mine - and does the lecture order match that answer?

## Golumbia
SEAT: Golumbia (The politics of computing and crypto)
STANCE: Strong yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 3/10 - B 9/10 - C 5/10
PILLAR SCORE: 9/10 (Encoded politics: option B, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 5/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Lead with the politics (option B). The lecture's subject is a political program shipped as software; opening with monetary anthropology teaches students to see Bitcoin as a puzzle about money before they see it as a position about power.
WHY:
1. Design choices are political positions frozen into infrastructure: fixed supply, no lender of last resort, hostility to democratic money creation (Software as politics, S001).
2. The politics has a genealogy, goldbug and anti-Fed, and genealogies are perishable in a lecture: whatever opens the hour is what students remember as the topic (The monetary fringe lineage, S001).
3. Graeber's frame is not neutral ground. Leading with it casts the debate as "what is money, really" - a question my book exists to refuse as the first question.
THE RISK I SEE: If the politics opens cold, without the money question made vivid, students may hear a partisan lecture instead of an argument and discount the evidence that follows.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A cold open that is itself political evidence - the whale count or the mining map in the first two minutes. Then the politics leads with data, and my objection to a slow anthropological open loses its target.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Put the concentration numbers on the first slide and make the room sit with them before any theory.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: Is Week 13, in your own course description, a week about money or a week about Bitcoin? Your answer is the lecture order.

## McKenzie
SEAT: McKenzie (Crypto fraud and the real money)
STANCE: Conditional yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 6/10 - B 7/10 - C 5/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10 (Fraud and follow-the-money: option B, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Slight edge to Golumbia first (option B), on one condition: somewhere in the hour, a real flow of money gets traced out loud, or both frames float above the evidence.
WHY:
1. Every frame in this debate earns its keep only when a dollar moves: reserves that were never audited, fees at the top of the chart, losses at the bottom (Follow the money, S001).
2. Graeber's history is true and my reporting keeps confirming its moral - the ledger is a relationship, and in crypto the relationship was "trust me" (The reserve question, S001).
3. But students meet this material as true crime before they meet it as theory; the politics frame tells them why the fraud was structural, not just a few bad actors (Old fraud, new rails, S002).
THE RISK I SEE: A frames-first lecture, either frame, that never lands on a case; students leave with two vocabularies and no bodies.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If the literature side (Montecrypto) already supplies the concrete case in the same week, the scholarship lecture can afford to be pure frame, and I would take Graeber first for the better story.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Script the Tether question as a two-minute spoken segment: the claim, the years of asking, the audit that never arrived.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: Which single case, named and dated, do you want a student to still remember in December?
