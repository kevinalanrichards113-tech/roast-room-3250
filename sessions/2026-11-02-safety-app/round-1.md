# Round 1 - Opening positions

## Zuboff
SEAT: Zuboff (Surveillance and power)
STANCE: Strong yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 1/10 - B 3/10 - C 9/10
PILLAR SCORE: 9/10 (Power and surveillance: option C, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 5/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Don't run it (option C); a city that flags behavior from phone and camera data has built an instrument for tuning citizens, whatever it calls the purpose.
WHY:
1. Follow the surplus first: location, movement, association and routine are taken far beyond any single safety incident, and that excess is the product (Behavioral surplus, S002).
2. The power here is instrumentarian, not persuasion: it works by cues, friction and intervention on behavior at scale, indifferent to what the flagged person believes (Instrumentarian power, S003).
3. The proposal cannot answer the three questions in democratic form: Who knows? The vendor and the city. Who decides? The model's thresholds. Who decides who decides? No one the resident can name (The three questions, S003).
THE RISK I SEE: Option B is the dispossession cycle in polite dress: publish a rate, add an appeal desk, habituate the city to being sensed, then expand the floor (The dispossession cycle, S002).
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A system that takes no surplus, keeps data under democratic control with a real right to sanctuary, and can be switched off by the people it senses.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Have students run the three questions on the app in one minute, out loud, before any debate about accuracy.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: Who owns the exhaust this app creates, and what does that ownership buy them over residents who never opted in?

## Sumpter
SEAT: Sumpter (Algorithms and profiling)
STANCE: Yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 2/10 - B 6/10 - C 8/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10 (Model mechanics and profiling: option C, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Don't run it on the numbers offered (option C), because no numbers were offered; B is the only version that could ever be tested.
WHY:
1. Mechanics before theory: name the model, the inputs, the objective, and the error rates before anyone philosophizes about safety (Profiling and the filter, S001).
2. Run the base rates out loud. Suspicious behavior that warrants intervention is rare, so even a model that looks accurate will produce mostly false flags; that arithmetic, not the demo, decides this case (The data manifesto / audit stance, S002).
3. Ask who sits in the false positive cell. Profiling acts on the category, not the person, and the category is one the resident cannot see, appeal, or escape (Profiling and the filter, S001).
THE RISK I SEE: A score without an error budget is an accusation, and at city scale the wrongly flagged will outnumber the rightly flagged by a wide margin.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Published false positive rates by group, worked against real base rates, showing true flags are not swamped, plus a lift over the dumb baseline of ordinary reports.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Have students compute one base rate problem by hand: 10,000 residents, a rare event, a good sounding accuracy, then count the false flags.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: Will the class be given the confusion matrix, or only the vendor's word that the app works?

## Lu
SEAT: Lu (Opacity and accountability)
STANCE: Conditional yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 1/10 - B 7/10 - C 6/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10 (Opacity and disclosure: option B, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Run it only as B, and only if B is specified by type: published error rates and an appeal path are a start, not a remedy.
WHY:
1. Classify before you cure. This case holds all three opacities at once: technical complexity in the model, trade secret protection for the vendor, and managerial hiding by the city (The three opacities, S001).
2. Option A fails every type at once. Option C avoids the harm but also avoids building any disclosure duty a future system would have to meet (Regulatory instruments, S002).
3. Accountability requires an addressee. Unless a named official must answer for a flag, the appeal path is managerial opacity wearing technical dress (Accountability requires an addressee, S002).
THE RISK I SEE: B as written in one line is underspecified, and an underspecified safeguard is how secrecy survives a transparency vote.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If the city cannot compel tiered disclosure from the vendor, or cannot name the accountable official, B collapses into A and I move to C.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Give students the three boxes and make them sort this app into each box before they vote.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: In B, who is answerable by name when a flag is wrong: a person, an office, or the system?

## Bender & Hanna
SEAT: Bender & Hanna (AI hype and its costs)
STANCE: Strong yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 1/10 - B 3/10 - C 9/10
PILLAR SCORE: 9/10 (Hype, extraction, and labor: option C, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 5/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Don't run it (option C). Translate the pitch first: a statistical pattern matcher over phone and camera data, sold with the verb "flags" so no human has to say "accuses".
WHY:
1. The con works through asymmetry: the city buys a system it cannot inspect, from a seller who profits from that blindness (The AI Con, S001).
2. Say what it does. The system does not understand suspicious behavior; it matches patterns in data, and the humans who labeled, moderated and will clean up its errors are hidden inside the word automatic (Say what it does, S002; The labor underneath, S003).
3. Stratification is the product: quick automated suspicion for the many, careful human judgment for those who can buy their way out of the queue (Slop and stratification, S001).
THE RISK I SEE: B launders the con. Publishing a rate the buyer cannot verify, and an appeal the flagged person must work, turns a sales story into a legitimacy story.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A narrow, verified utility that survives translation: a named task, inspected by the buyer, with the hidden labor priced and the capability claim stated in mechanical terms.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Have students rewrite the app pitch in mechanical sentences, with the vendor's verbs removed.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: What can the city actually inspect before it signs, and who told the city this counts as safety?

## Graeber
SEAT: Graeber (Debt, money, and social relations)
STANCE: Yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 1/10 - B 4/10 - C 8/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10 (Money as social relation: option C, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Don't run it (option C); look at the ledger it creates, and ask who ends up owing what to whom.
WHY:
1. Every ledger has an author. Here the city and its vendor write the ledger of suspicion, and the resident discovers an entry against their name only after it has been acted on (Credit versus bullion, S002, applied to a ledger of flags).
2. The appeal path in B is a moralized debt in modern dress: the flagged person now owes the system proof of innocence, and must pay in time, documents and deference (Primordial debt, S001, applied by analogy).
3. The everyday trust that actually runs a city, neighbors assuming good faith until shown otherwise, is the substrate this app taxes without ever naming (Baseline communism, S003).
THE RISK I SEE: Once suspicion is ledgered, it compounds. A flag follows a person across services the way a bad debt record does, long after the original entry was wrong.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A ledger the flagged person can read, contest and have erased, written under rules they had a hand in making.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Ask students a simple historical question: in Black's Hollerith case, who wrote the ledger, and who was written into it?
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: If a student is flagged, what does the system say they owe, and to whom do they pay it?

## Golumbia
SEAT: Golumbia (The politics of computing and crypto)
STANCE: Strong yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 1/10 - B 3/10 - C 9/10
PILLAR SCORE: 9/10 (Encoded politics: option C, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Don't run it (option C); read the politics off the design, not off the brochure.
WHY:
1. Design choices are political positions frozen into infrastructure: continuous sensing, a suspicion threshold set by officials, and no resident discretion over being scored are positions, not neutral features (Software as politics, S001).
2. The claim to be above politics is the politics' favorite costume. Safety talk here does the same work cyberlibertarian talk does elsewhere: it re-describes a governing program as a technical fact (Cyberlibertarianism, S002).
3. Audit the democratization claim with counts, as with any such system: who is watched most, who is watched least, and who holds the watching power is the real constitution of this app (Concentration as refutation, S001, applied to sensing power).
THE RISK I SEE: B proceduralizes the politics without changing it. Error rates and appeals govern how the program runs; they do not ask whether suspicion as a mode of governance should run at all.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Evidence that the politics is actually thin here: plural governance, resident control over the thresholds, and a design that makes whole categories of monitoring impossible, not merely reviewable.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Have students read the app design like a party platform: what does it make easy, hard, and impossible, and for whom?
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: Whose theory of order is built into the word suspicious, and was it ever voted on?

## Butler
SEAT: Butler (Precarity and whose lives count)
STANCE: Strong yes (on the option I recommend)
OPTION SCORES: A 1/10 - B 4/10 - C 9/10
PILLAR SCORE: 9/10 (Precarity and grievability: option C, judged on my pillar alone)
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: one-way

THE CALL: Don't run it (option C); we might ask first whose exposure rises under this arrangement, and whose quietly falls.
WHY:
1. Precariousness is shared; precarity is its unequal distribution. A flag does not fall evenly: those already most exposed to police, landlords and employers will bear its costs first and longest (Precarity vs. precariousness, S001).
2. The frame decides before any officer does. To be entered as suspicious is to be framed as a risk before one is recognized as a life, and losses inside that frame register as administration, not harm (Frames and grievability, S002).
3. Categories are performative. What a system repeatedly calls a person becomes part of what that person is, socially and materially, in every office that reads the file (Performativity and norms, S003).
THE RISK I SEE: The appeal in B asks the most precarious person to do the most work to be re-framed as grievable, with the least time, counsel and security to do it.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A showing that the arrangement lowers exposure for the most exposed first, and that a person can fail to match their file and survive the mismatch.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Ask students to name, for one flag, whose week gets harder and whose gets easier. Keep the names concrete.
QUESTION FOR THE INSTRUCTOR: When a flag is wrong, whose loss will this class count as a loss, and whose will it process as a statistic?

## Chair notes

- **Crux 1 - Are published rates and an appeal a remedy or a legitimizer?** Lu says B is the only option that builds a disclosure duty, if it is specified by opacity type. Zuboff, Bender & Hanna, Golumbia and Butler say B as written launders the system: it governs how suspicion runs without asking whether it should run, and it shifts work onto the flagged. Sumpter sits between: B is the only testable version, and the test has not been run.
- **Crux 2 - Does the base rate arithmetic kill the app on its own terms?** Sumpter says rare events plus city scale mean false flags swamp true flags, and no one has produced the confusion matrix. Bender & Hanna press the prior question: the numbers, if produced, come from the seller the buyer cannot inspect. Zuboff and Golumbia say even good arithmetic would not settle ownership or encoded politics.
- **Crux 3 - Who bears the cost of being wrong?** Butler and Graeber say the flagged resident pays: exposure in Butler's frame, a debt of proof in Graeber's ledger. Lu must answer whether a named accountable official and an inspectable appeal change that distribution, or merely describe it better.
